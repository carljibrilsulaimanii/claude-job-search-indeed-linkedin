# Application Questions, Cover Letters, and Screening Fields

Read this when the person has picked a role and the application asks for more than a resume.

## Answer the question they actually asked

Many postings end with a specific prompt — "describe one referral relationship you built from nothing," "why do you want to join our community," "tell us about your experience with X." Most applicants send a generic cover letter and ignore it. Answering the literal prompt is itself a differentiator, and ignoring it reads as mass-applying.

Respect stated word limits. If they say 300 words, come in under 300 and say so implicitly by being tight.

## Default answer format

Unless the person or the form says otherwise:

- First person, plain language, paragraph form
- Under the character limit. Forms often cap at 1,500 or 500 characters, so count.
- No em dashes, and no bullets unless the question asks for them. When it does ("1–3 bullet points"), open with a one-sentence summary, then the bullets.
- Include the person's portfolio link where it fits, if they have one in the standing record.
- When the person wants a reusable answer, leave the company and role names out.

**Employment-history fields** ("current employer, title, dates, reason for leaving") get one employer per field. Use exact titles and dates from the standing record, and give a reason for leaving that's honest and respects the don't-mention list.

**Proficiency ratings and "years of X"** are scored on the specific platform named, not related tools. Rate honestly.

**Requests for a former employer's confidential data** (client case studies, internal numbers): decline politely, say why, and offer an alternative such as anonymized results, rounded ratios, or a walkthrough of the approach.

**A required degree the person doesn't have:** write a short first-person reason for applying anyway. Name the experience that substitutes, and don't apologize.

## Mine the posting for its own language

The posting tells you what it's screening for. Answer with their vocabulary, using the person's real experience.

Concrete patterns worth catching:

- A posting saying "referrals are usually won on the fourth contact, not the first" is screening for follow-up discipline. The answer should mention persistence over months, not enthusiasm.
- A posting asking for someone who explains "which patients we're right for, and which we are not" is screening for honesty over salesmanship. Give an example of turning something down.
- A posting that lists "community health, case management, or social services" as acceptable experience is offering an alternate qualification path. Name that category explicitly so the reviewer can check the box rather than having to infer it.

## The gap paragraph

When the person doesn't meet a stated requirement, address it in the application rather than hoping it goes unnoticed.

Volunteering a limitation honestly reads as more credible than a list of strengths. A working shape: name the gap plainly, then name the closest true evidence that they'll close it. "I don't have SAP experience, but I learned three HRIS platforms on the job with no formal training and would expect invoice processing to follow the same pattern."

Do this once, near the end. More than once sounds apologetic.

## Overqualification

When someone applies to a role below their level — often deliberately, for stability or a foot in the door — the reviewer's real fear is that they'll leave in six months.

Address it directly: say why *this* role is the one they want, and tie it to something specific in the posting (an advancement path, a mission, a team). Silence on this point gets read as the candidate not having noticed.

## Salary expectation fields

If the posting states a range, defer to it. If it doesn't, give a researched range with a short reason, then signal flexibility. Naming a number shows seriousness; refusing to name one on a form that requires it can drop the application.

For hourly-to-salary roles, address both.

## Screening questions with a hidden purpose

Short factual fields often carry more weight than they appear to:

- "How many years of X" — answer with the honest count, but count generously and correctly. Self-employment, side businesses, and duties inside another role all count if the person genuinely did the work.
- Location and onsite-availability questions are frequently hard filters. Answer plainly and confirm the commute is real before saying yes.
- Unusual questions ("do you play chess, and at what level") are culture probes. Answer honestly and briefly, and connect it to something about how the person works.

## Voice

Write the way the person talks, not the way a cover letter template talks. Short sentences. Plain words. "Got me nowhere" over "did not yield results."

Match the employer's register: a posting written with jokes and personality should not receive corporate prose, and a government or clinical posting should not receive slang.

## Before sending

Read it back as the hiring manager and check three things: does it answer the actual prompt, is every fact in it true and defensible in an interview, and does it say anything a hundred other applicants couldn't say. If the last answer is no, find the specific detail only this person has.

**Flag any invented specifics to the person before they send.** Any illustrative detail added for narrative flow — a named client, a described conversation, a number — must be confirmed as true. They will be asked to expand on it.
